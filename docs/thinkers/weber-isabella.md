# Isabella Weber

German economist, born in Nuremberg in 1987, on the economics faculty of the University of Massachusetts Amherst since 2019, where she is also research leader for China at the Political Economy Research Institute (PERI). She studied at the Free University of Berlin and Peking University and holds two doctorates: a PhD in Development Studies from the University of Cambridge (2018, supervised by Peter Nolan) and a PhD in Economics from the New School for Social Research (2019). She taught at Goldsmiths, University of London, from 2017 to 2019.

Her Cambridge thesis became *How China Escaped Shock Therapy: The Market Reform Debate* (2021). The book reconstructs the Chinese debate of the 1980s over price reform. One camp, influenced by Eastern European reformers and visiting Western economists, proposed a "big bang" liberalization of all prices at once. The other favored a dual-track system in which state-set prices for planned quotas coexisted with market prices at the margin, so that markets were introduced gradually from the edges of the planned economy. Weber argues that the leadership's rejection of the big-bang option in the 1980s is a main reason China's reform path differed from Russia's in the 1990s. She traces Chinese approaches to price stabilization to the *Guanzi*, with its distinction between "heavy" goods the state should regulate and "light" marginal goods, to the ever-normal granary used to smooth grain prices, and to the Communist Party's market interventions against inflation in the 1940s.

In December 2021 Weber published a *Guardian* op-ed proposing strategic price controls as part of the response to post-pandemic inflation, citing [[galbraith-john-kenneth]]'s account of Second World War price control and the Office of Price Administration. [[krugman-paul]] responded on Twitter, "I am not a free-market zealot. But this is truly stupid," then deleted the post and apologized. Her paper with Evan Wasner, "Sellers' Inflation, Profits and Conflict" (*Review of Keynesian Economics*, 2023), argues that US pandemic-era inflation was predominantly a "sellers' inflation": a shared cost shock allowed firms with market power to raise prices together without explicit coordination, and profits rose as a result. With Jesus Lara Jauregui, Lucas Teixeira, and Luiza Nassif Pires she developed the idea of "systemically significant prices," using an input-output model of the US economy to identify eight sectors (including petroleum, oil and gas extraction, utilities, farms, food, and housing) whose price shocks propagate most widely. In February 2022 she and Sebastian Dullien proposed a two-tier gas price for German households, subsidizing a base quota of consumption while leaving market prices at the margin. A government-appointed gas commission proposed a similar structure in October 2022, and Germany introduced a gas price brake (*Gaspreisbremse*) for 2023.

## Key ideas

- Dual-track price reform as an alternative to shock therapy
- Price stabilization as a long-standing function of the Chinese state
- Sellers' inflation: profit-led price increases in concentrated sectors during supply shocks
- Systemically significant prices and targeted price stabilization
- Strategic price controls as an emergency tool

## Reception

### Agreement

- **James K. Galbraith** defended the proposal in "The Case for Strategic Price Policies" (Project Syndicate, January 2022), arguing that US price policy had historically served both emergency and peacetime coordination functions and that claims it had always failed were inaccurate.
- **Economic Policy Institute** analysis estimated that profits accounted for about a third of US nonfinancial-corporate price growth from 2020 Q2 to 2022 Q4, against about 13 percent in prior periods.
- **IMF** staff (Hansen, Toscani, and Zhou, 2023) attributed about 45 percent of the rise in the euro-area consumption deflator from early 2022 to mid-2023 to profits.
- **Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City** researchers (Glover, Mustre-del-Río, and von Ende-Becker, 2023) estimated that markups grew about 3.4 percent in 2021, more than half of that year's 5.8 percent PCE inflation.
- **Paul Krugman**, after the initial dismissal, later wrote in the *New York Times* that price controls may be a useful part of inflation management (as reported by the History News Network).
- *How China Escaped Shock Therapy* received favorable reviews in *Pacific Affairs* and *Boston Review*.

### Disagreement

- **Free-market critics** opposed price controls on principle. *Reason* (January 2022) called them the worst possible response to inflation. Ryan Bourne of the Cato Institute argues that Weber turns a partial mechanism into a general theory, and that executives' references to "pricing power" do not establish it.
- **Commentators in the policy mainstream** questioned the novelty or durability of the claims. Noah Smith argued that it was "too early for a victory lap" on price controls; Matthew Yglesias questioned whether "sellers' inflation" added anything to standard demand-pull accounts.
- **Empirical studies finding a smaller profit role**: a Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond brief (2023) concluded that markup changes did not drive US inflation; a study of Canadian firms found markup changes accounted for less than a tenth of 2021 inflation; a SUERF policy note on the euro area characterized elevated margins as a mostly temporary pass-through of input costs rather than a sign of weak competition.
- **On the China book**, a review on the ACAES Asia economics blog calls it carefully researched but argues that it defines shock therapy too narrowly, as rapid price liberalization plus monetary austerity.

### Schools of thought

This section maps the positions above onto schools of thought; the mapping is a synthesis, not a single source's claim. Weber's work belongs to the heterodox tradition centered at UMass Amherst, the New School, and the *Review of Keynesian Economics*: post-Keynesian theories of markup pricing and of inflation as distributional conflict between wages and profits, institutionalist accounts of administered prices and wartime price control associated with [[galbraith-john-kenneth]], and [[polanyi-karl]]'s treatment of markets as embedded in state institutions. Supporters cluster in this tradition and in progressive policy organizations (EPI, the Roosevelt Institute, with which she is affiliated). Opposition from Cato and *Reason* follows the free-market tradition of [[hayek-fa]] and [[friedman-milton]], in which price controls distort the information prices carry and inflation is a monetary or excess-demand phenomenon. Mainstream New Keynesian economists, including Krugman initially, generally attributed the 2021–22 inflation to demand and supply shocks working through standard channels; the dispute with them concerns the size of the profit contribution and whether rising profit shares were a cause or a by-product of input-cost pass-through.

### Standing

Weber was named to TIME100 Next (2023), *Capital*'s "Top 40 under 40" (2022), and *FOCUS* magazine's 100 Women of the Year in Germany (2023), and was nominated for *Prospect*'s 2024 World's Top Thinkers list, where the magazine wrote that she "can make a good claim to being one of the more influential economists of the past few years." A *New Yorker* profile described her movement from "the most hated woman in economics," following the 2021 op-ed, to wider prominence. She has testified as an expert before the German Bundestag and holds affiliations with Harvard's Fairbank Center and the Berggruen Institute.
