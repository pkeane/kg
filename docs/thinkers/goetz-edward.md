# Edward G. Goetz

American urban planning scholar, professor at the Humphrey School of Public Affairs and director of the Center for Urban and Regional Affairs at the University of Minnesota. His subject is American housing policy and the way race and poverty structure it — specifically the sequence by which the federal government built public housing, concentrated poor and predominantly Black tenants in it, and then demolished it.

*Shelter Burden: Local Politics and Progressive Housing Policy* (Temple, 1993) examines the municipal politics of housing provision, and why local governments with progressive housing commitments produce the outcomes they do. *Clearing the Way: Deconcentrating the Poor in Urban America* (Urban Institute Press, 2003) documents the deconcentration turn: the shift in policy from providing housing units to dispersing the residents of concentrated poverty, and the assumptions about neighborhood effects that justified it.

*New Deal Ruins: Race, Economic Justice, and Public Housing Policy* (Cornell, 2013) is the central book. It traces the dismantling of American public housing from the 1990s onward — the HOPE VI program, authorized in 1992, together with tenant-based vouchers and mixed-income redevelopment — and accounts for the results: a loss of more than 250,000 permanently affordable units and the displacement of hundreds of thousands of residents, with outcomes for the displaced ranging from mixed to severe. Case chapters cover Chicago, Atlanta, and New Orleans; one chapter is titled "'Negro Removal' Revisited," locating the demolitions in continuity with the urban renewal of the 1950s and 1960s rather than as a break from it. Goetz argues that demolition and redevelopment served local gentrification objectives, and that the political case against public housing was not settled by evidence that public housing had failed.

*The One-Way Street of Integration: Fair Housing and the Pursuit of Racial Justice in American Cities* (Cornell, 2018) turns to the conflict inside fair-housing politics between integration and community development. Integration as pursued in American policy has been one-directional — the movement of low-income residents of color out of their neighborhoods — rather than reciprocal, and it has competed for resources and legitimacy with strategies that invest in those neighborhoods and build political power in place. Goetz treats the disagreement as substantive rather than as a failure of will on either side.

## Key themes

- *Deconcentration* — the policy turn from providing units to dispersing residents, and the neighborhood-effects research invoked to justify it
- *Demolition as continuity* — HOPE VI read as the successor to mid-century urban renewal rather than a departure from it
- *The displaced as the unit of analysis* — evaluating housing policy by what happened to the people removed, not by indicators for the neighborhood or city they left
- *Integration versus community development* — two irreconcilable strategies inside fair-housing politics, and the asymmetry in whose neighborhoods are expected to change
