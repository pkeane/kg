# Jay Forrester

American electrical engineer and founder of system dynamics, the modeling method that treats social and economic systems as networks of stocks, flows, feedback loops, and delays whose behavior over time can be simulated. He came to the social sciences from control engineering and computing, and the transfer of servomechanism concepts — feedback, gain, lag — into organizational and economic analysis is his central methodological move.

Forrester was born on a cattle ranch near Anselmo, Nebraska, took a degree in electrical engineering at the University of Nebraska in 1939, and joined MIT's Servomechanisms Laboratory. He directed Project Whirlwind, one of the first real-time digital computers, and in the late 1940s invented coincident-current random-access magnetic-core memory, which remained the dominant computer memory technology into the 1970s; he also led computer work for the SAGE air-defense system. He moved to the MIT Sloan School of Management in 1956, where he spent the rest of his career.

*Industrial Dynamics* (1961) applied feedback modeling to production and distribution, including the amplification of demand variability along supply chains later known as the bullwhip or Forrester effect; the MIT "Beer Game" teaching exercise derives from it. *Urban Dynamics* (1969) modeled a city as an interacting system of housing, business, and population stocks. Its measures of urban health are city-level ratios — jobs per resident, tax base per capita, rate of upward mobility — and its governing mechanism is attractiveness: a policy that improves conditions for poor residents raises in-migration, which returns the ratios to their prior equilibrium. Job training and low-cost housing construction therefore yield no lasting improvement in the model, while demolition of low-income housing does, since it reduces the relevant population stock and frees land for business. Forrester had no training in urban studies; the domain input came from John Collins, former mayor of Boston, who held the office next to his at MIT from 1968. The book drew sustained criticism from planners and social scientists, most directly Gray, Pessel, and Varaiya's demonstration that the model excludes commuting across the city boundary and any effect of the city on the wider society, so that displaced residents simply leave the simulation. Forrester did not revise the conclusions: with Nathaniel Mass he answered the RAND critique in "Urban Dynamics: A Rejoinder to Averch and Levine" (1973), and the MIT group extended the program through *Readings in Urban Dynamics* (1974–75) and Louis Alfeld and Alan Graham's *Introduction to Urban Dynamics* (1976) before Forrester turned to the National Model. [[meadows-donella|Donella Meadows]] restated the housing conclusion approvingly in "Leverage Points" (1997/1999) and in *Thinking in Systems* (2008, p. 146), where the subsequent demolition of public-housing projects is cited as confirmation; this is how most present-day readers encounter it. His general claim, stated in "Counterintuitive Behavior of Social Systems" (1971), is that complex systems respond to intervention in ways that contradict intuition, so policies aimed at symptoms often make matters worse.

*World Dynamics* (1971) presented World2, a global model of population, capital, resources, food, and pollution built after Forrester's contact with Aurelio Peccei and the Club of Rome. [[meadows-donella|Donella Meadows]], Dennis Meadows, and their MIT team extended it into World3, the basis of *The Limits to Growth* (1972). Forrester spent his later decades on the National Model of the US economy and on the System Dynamics in Education Project, which sought to introduce the method into secondary schooling.

## Key ideas

- *System dynamics* — simulation of social systems as stock-and-flow structures with feedback and delay
- *Counterintuitive behavior of social systems* — the mismatch between intuitive policy and actual system response
- *Structure determines behavior* — persistent patterns are properties of a system's loop structure, not of external events
- *Bullwhip (Forrester) effect* — demand variability amplified upstream through a distribution chain

Levine, *Two Models of the Urban Crisis: An Analytical Essay on Banfield and Forrester* (RAND RM-6366, 1970) — argues the model's argument and evidence are inadequate to support its policy implications. Forrester and Nathaniel Mass replied in "Urban Dynamics: A Rejoinder to Averch and Levine" (1973).

- Louis Alfeld, "Urban Dynamics — The First Fifty Years," *System Dynamics Review* 11:3 (1995), and Jay Forrester, "System Dynamics — A Personal View of the First Fifty Years," *System Dynamics Review* 23:2–3 (2007) — the two retrospectives from inside the school; not consulted here, and the place to look for any later revision of the urban conclusions.
- William Nordhaus, "World Dynamics: Measurement Without Data," *Economic Journal* 83:332 (1973) — the economists' case against World2: functional forms and parameters asserted rather than estimated.
- Kevin T. Baker, "Model Metropolis," *Logic* 6 (2018) — the political career of *Urban Dynamics*, from Moynihan and the Nixon White House to *SimCity*.
