# Kate Raworth

English economist, author of *Doughnut Economics: Seven Ways to Think Like a 21st-Century Economist* (Random House Business, 2017), and originator of the "doughnut" diagram of social and planetary boundaries. She took a BA in philosophy, politics and economics and an MSc in development economics at Oxford, worked from 1995 to 1998 as an Overseas Development Institute fellow on micro-enterprise in Zanzibar, was an economist and co-author on the UN Development Programme's *Human Development Report* from 1997 to 2001, and was a senior researcher at Oxfam from 2002 to 2013. She is a senior associate at Oxford's Environmental Change Institute and, since 2020, professor of practice at the Amsterdam University of Applied Sciences.

The doughnut first appeared in an Oxfam discussion paper, "A Safe and Just Space for Humanity: Can We Live Within the Doughnut?" (2012). The diagram sets two concentric rings. The outer ring is an ecological ceiling built from the nine planetary boundaries proposed by Johan Rockström, Will Steffen, and colleagues at the Stockholm Resilience Centre in 2009: climate change, ocean acidification, chemical pollution, nitrogen and phosphorus loading, freshwater withdrawals, land conversion, biodiversity loss, air pollution, and ozone depletion. The inner ring is a social foundation of twelve dimensions drawn from the priorities of the UN Sustainable Development Goals: food, health, education, income and work, peace and justice, political voice, social equity, gender equality, housing, networks, energy, and water. The habitable ring between them — overshoot above, shortfall below — is the "safe and just space for humanity."

*Doughnut Economics* organizes the argument as seven reframings, each presented as a replacement for a diagram or assumption Raworth identifies as embedded in introductory economics teaching: change the goal (from GDP growth to the doughnut); see the big picture (from the self-contained market of the circular-flow diagram to an economy embedded in society and the biosphere, following [[polanyi-karl|Polanyi]]); nurture human nature (from rational economic man to socially adaptable humans); get savvy with systems (from mechanical equilibrium to dynamic complexity, drawing on [[meadows-donella|Donella Meadows]]); design to distribute (from the Kuznets curve's promise that growth eventually equalizes to distribution built into economic structure); create to regenerate (from the environmental Kuznets curve to regenerative design); and be agnostic about growth (from economies that need to grow whether or not they make people thrive to economies that make people thrive whether or not they grow). The book was longlisted for the Financial Times and McKinsey Business Book of the Year Award.

In 2019 Raworth co-founded the Doughnut Economics Action Lab (DEAL) with Carlota Sanz, an organization that develops tools for applying the framework at city and national scale. Amsterdam adopted a "city portrait" version in April 2020 as a frame for post-pandemic planning, developed with DEAL, Circle Economy, Biomimicry 3.8, and C40 Cities; downscaled doughnut analyses have since been produced for Scotland, Wales, the Netherlands, South Africa, and other jurisdictions. She was appointed to the WHO Council on the Economics of Health for All in 2021.

[[milanovic-branko|Branko Milanović]] reviewed the book critically on his *Global Inequality* blog in 2018 under the title "Kate Raworth's economics of miracles," arguing that bringing the world's population to a decent standard of living would require a large multiple of current global output, that Raworth's growth agnosticism does not confront this arithmetic, and that her account presumes a change in preferences away from commodification for which he sees no evidence. Raworth replied on her own site. The exchange is the standard reference point for the dispute between the ecological-limits position and the position that growth remains necessary for poor countries.

## Key ideas

- *The doughnut* — a ring bounded by an ecological ceiling of planetary boundaries and a social foundation of human deprivations, with the space between as the operating target for economies
- *Embedded economy* — the economy as a subsystem of society and the biosphere rather than a self-contained circular flow
- *Growth agnosticism* — designing economies that can deliver on the social foundation without depending on continuous GDP growth, as distinct from a positive commitment to degrowth
- *Distributive and regenerative by design* — treating equity and ecological restoration as features of economic structure rather than as outcomes that growth will produce later
- *Diagrams as pedagogy* — the claim that the images in introductory textbooks (circular flow, supply and demand, the Kuznets curve, Rostow's stages of growth) carry the discipline's assumptions and are what most need replacing
